# Angular Momentum Algebra

Angular momentum is where quantum mechanics turns symmetry into spectra. Rotational invariance does not merely say that space has no preferred direction; it forces commutation relations, multiplets, selection rules, and the labels used for atoms, nuclei, and spin systems.

Sakurai presents angular momentum as the representation theory of rotations, with spin and orbital angular momentum treated under the same algebra. Ballentine gives a rigorous operator and rotation treatment, including finite rotations and tensor operators. The Gottfried-named notes develop the algebra after spin-1/2 and symmetries. Schiff's older coordinate style emphasizes orbital angular momentum, spherical harmonics, and central-potential wave functions.

## The algebra behind rotations

An angular momentum operator $\mathbf J=(J_x,J_y,J_z)$ satisfies

$$
[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k.
$$

The square is

$$
J^2=J_x^2+J_y^2+J_z^2.
$$

It commutes with all components:

$$
[J^2,J_i]=0.
$$

Therefore one can choose simultaneous eigenstates of $J^2$ and $J_z$:

$$
J^2|j,m\rangle=\hbar^2j(j+1)|j,m\rangle,
$$

and

$$
J_z|j,m\rangle=\hbar m|j,m\rangle.
$$

The ladder operators are

$$
J_+=J_x+iJ_y,\qquad J_-=J_x-iJ_y.
$$

They obey

$$
[J_z,J_\pm]=\pm\hbar J_\pm,
$$

and

$$
[J_+,J_-]=2\hbar J_z.
$$

For orbital angular momentum,

$$
\mathbf L=\mathbf R\times \mathbf P,
$$

with coordinate-space components such as

$$
L_z=-i\hbar{\partial\over \partial \phi}.
$$

Spin angular momentum obeys the same algebra but is not generated by a particle's spatial orbit. For spin-1/2,

$$
\mathbf S={\hbar\over 2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}.
$$

## Quantization from ladder norms

The allowed quantum numbers are

$$
j=0,{1\over 2},1,{3\over 2},\ldots,
\qquad
m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j-1,j.
$$

The ladder action is

$$
J_\pm|j,m\rangle
=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,|j,m\pm1\rangle.
$$

This formula encodes both the spacing of $m$ and the endpoint condition $J_+\vert j,j\rangle=0$, $J_-\vert j,-j\rangle=0$.

The coefficient follows without choosing a coordinate representation. First note that

$$
J_-J_+=J^2-J_z^2-\hbar J_z,
$$

and similarly $J_+J_-=J^2-J_z^2+\hbar J_z$. If $J_+\vert j,m\rangle=C_+\vert j,m+1\rangle$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
|C_+|^2
&=\langle j,m|J_-J_+|j,m\rangle\\
&=\hbar^2[j(j+1)-m(m+1)].
\end{aligned}
$$

Choosing real positive ladder coefficients gives the stated formula. The analogous calculation gives the coefficient of $J_-$. Since a norm cannot be negative, upward steps must end at some $m_{\max}$ and downward steps at $m_{\min}$. At the endpoints,

$$
j(j+1)=m_{\max}(m_{\max}+1)
=m_{\min}(m_{\min}-1).
$$

Thus $m_{\max}=j$, $m_{\min}=-j$, and the number of unit steps between them is $2j$. That number is a nonnegative integer, so $j$ is an integer or half-integer. The familiar spectrum follows from positivity and finite-dimensionality rather than from a semiclassical picture of a rotating vector.

The components cannot all be simultaneously sharp. In $\vert j,m\rangle$,

$$
\langle J_x\rangle=\langle J_y\rangle=0,
$$

and

$$
\langle J_x^2+J_y^2\rangle=\hbar^2[j(j+1)-m^2].
$$

The state has a known length and known $z$ projection but no sharp transverse components. Symmetry around the $z$ axis gives $\langle J_x^2\rangle=\langle J_y^2\rangle$, hence

$$
(\Delta J_x)^2=(\Delta J_y)^2
={\hbar^2\over2}[j(j+1)-m^2].
$$

For the stretched state $m=j$, this becomes $\hbar^2j/2$. It saturates

$$
\Delta J_x\,\Delta J_y\geq {\hbar\over2}|\langle J_z\rangle|.
$$

Finite rotations are generated by angular momentum:

$$
U(R_{\mathbf n}(\theta))=\exp\left(-{i\theta\over \hbar}\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf J\right).
$$

For an infinitesimal rotation about $\mathbf n$,

$$
U(\delta\theta)=1-{i\delta\theta\over\hbar}\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf J+O(\delta\theta^2).
$$

Conjugating a vector operator $\mathbf V$ and comparing with its geometric infinitesimal rotation yields

$$
[J_i,V_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}V_k.
$$

Taking $\mathbf V=\mathbf J$ recovers the angular-momentum commutators. The algebra is therefore the local, infinitesimal form of rotational covariance.

For orbital angular momentum, the angular wave functions are spherical harmonics:

$$
L^2Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\phi),
$$

and

$$
L_zY_\ell^m(\theta,\phi)=\hbar mY_\ell^m(\theta,\phi).
$$

The algebra fixes the allowed $\ell$ and $m$ before any central-potential radial equation is solved. Coordinate wave mechanics supplies an additional fact: scalar orbital wavefunctions are single-valued under $\phi\mapsto\phi+2\pi$, so their $m$ values are integers.

|---|---|---|
| Commutator | $[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$ | defines angular momentum |
| Casimir | $J^2$ | labels irreducible multiplet |
| Projection | $J_z$ | labels state inside multiplet |

## Worked example: ladder coefficients for $j=1$

**Problem.** Compute $J_-\vert 1,1\rangle$ and $J_-\vert 1,0\rangle$.

**Method.**

1. Use

$$
J_-|j,m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m-1)}\,|j,m-1\rangle.
$$

2. For $j=1$, $m=1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_-|1,1\rangle
&=\hbar\sqrt{1(1+1)-1(1-1)}\,|1,0\rangle\\
&=\hbar\sqrt{2}\,|1,0\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

3. For $j=1$, $m=0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_-|1,0\rangle
&=\hbar\sqrt{1(1+1)-0(0-1)}\,|1,-1\rangle\\
&=\hbar\sqrt{2}\,|1,-1\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

4. At the bottom,

$$
J_-|1,-1\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{2-(-1)(-2)}\,|1,-2\rangle=0,
$$

so there is no physical $\vert 1,-2\rangle$ state.

**Checked answer.** The $j=1$ multiplet has exactly three states: $m=1,0,-1$.

## Worked example: constructing the spin-1 matrices

**Problem.** In the ordered basis $\{\vert 1,1\rangle,\vert 1,0\rangle,\vert 1,-1\rangle\}$, find $J_x$.

**Method.**

1. Build $J_+$ from ladder actions:

$$
J_+|1,-1\rangle=\hbar\sqrt2|1,0\rangle,
\qquad
J_+|1,0\rangle=\hbar\sqrt2|1,1\rangle,
\qquad
J_+|1,1\rangle=0.
$$

2. Therefore

$$
J_+=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}
0&\sqrt2&0\\
0&0&\sqrt2\\
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

3. Since $J_-=J_+^\dagger$,

$$
J_-=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&0\\
\sqrt2&0&0\\
0&\sqrt2&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

4. Use

$$
J_x={1\over 2}(J_++J_-).
$$

5. Thus

$$
J_x={\hbar\over \sqrt2}
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
1&0&1\\
0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Checked answer.** The matrix is Hermitian, as an observable must be, and it couples only neighboring $m$ states.

## Checking a matrix representation numerically

```python
import numpy as np

def angular_momentum_matrices(j, hbar=1.0):
    m_vals = np.arange(j, -j - 1, -1)
    dim = len(m_vals)
    jp = np.zeros((dim, dim), dtype=complex)
    for col, m in enumerate(m_vals):
        mp = m + 1
        if mp <= j:
            row = np.where(np.isclose(m_vals, mp))[0]
            if len(row):
                jp[row[0], col] = hbar * np.sqrt(j * (j + 1) - m * (m + 1))
    jm = jp.conj().T
    jx = 0.5 * (jp + jm)
    jy = (jp - jm) / (2j)
    jz = np.diag(hbar * m_vals)
    return jx, jy, jz

jx, jy, jz = angular_momentum_matrices(1)
print(jx)
print(np.allclose(jx @ jy - jy @ jx, 1j * jz))
```

## Where the notation tends to slip

- Treating $\mathbf J$ as an ordinary vector with simultaneously known components. Only $J^2$ and one component can be sharp together.
- Forgetting half-integer $j$ values. Spin permits representations that orbital angular momentum alone does not.
- Losing endpoint conditions. The ladder formulas determine allowed $m$ values by requiring nonnegative norms.
- Confusing orbital $\ell$ with total $j$. In atoms with spin, total angular momentum combines orbital and spin parts.
- Using $J_\pm$ without the square-root coefficient. Transition strengths depend on it.
- Assuming $L_z=-i\hbar\partial_\phi$ applies to spin. Spin lives in an internal space, not in ordinary coordinate dependence.
- Ignoring conventions for spherical harmonics and phases. Clebsch-Gordan tables depend on consistent phase choices.

The cleanest way to avoid angular-momentum confusion is to identify which angular momentum is being discussed before writing equations. Orbital angular momentum $\mathbf L$ acts on spatial wave functions. Spin $\mathbf S$ acts on internal spin degrees of freedom. Total angular momentum $\mathbf J$ may mean $\mathbf L+\mathbf S$ or the sum of several subsystem angular momenta. They obey the same algebra but act on different spaces. The shared algebra is why Sakurai can treat them uniformly; the different physical meanings are why notation must stay explicit.

The ladder derivation also teaches a general quantum habit: norms must be nonnegative. The allowed $j$ and $m$ values are not guessed from geometry; they follow because repeated raising or lowering must stop before a state with negative norm appears. This is one of the places where Hilbert-space structure directly produces quantization. The same type of reasoning appears in the harmonic oscillator, where $a^\dagger a$ is positive and the ladder must terminate at the ground state.

For orbital angular momentum, the connection to wave mechanics is made through spherical harmonics. The equation $L_z=-i\hbar\partial_\phi$ implies angular dependence $e^{im\phi}$, and single-valuedness under $\phi\mapsto\phi+2\pi$ forces integer $m$ for orbital wave functions. Spin-$1/2$ does not arise from single-valued scalar spatial functions. Spinors transform under the double cover $SU(2)$ of the rotation group $SO(3)$, so a $2\pi$ rotation may multiply a state vector by $-1$ while leaving all physical rays unchanged.

Angular momentum is often more valuable as a labeling system than as a set of matrices. Once $j$ and $m$ are good quantum numbers, Hamiltonian blocks decouple and degeneracies become organized. Atomic structure, scattering partial waves, molecular rotation, and nuclear spin all use this bookkeeping. Ignoring the symmetry usually produces a larger matrix with the same block structure hidden inside it.

There is a small but important distinction between a state vector and a spatial vector. Under a $2\pi$ rotation, an integer-$j$ state returns to itself and a half-integer-$j$ state changes sign. Expectation values and probabilities remain unchanged in either case. A $4\pi$ rotation restores a half-integer state vector. Interference experiments can detect the relative sign when one branch is rotated and another is not, but no single isolated ray distinguishes $\vert \psi\rangle$ from $-\vert \psi\rangle$.

A final useful check is rotational covariance. If a result claims to describe a rotationally invariant Hamiltonian but depends on a particular $m$ value, either a direction has been introduced or the calculation has broken symmetry by mistake. Conversely, when an external magnetic field is present, dependence on $m$ is expected because the field defines a preferred axis. Asking "what picked the $z$ axis?" is a simple way to catch incorrect degeneracy splitting.

In longer calculations, keep commutators and eigenvalue equations separate. The commutators define the algebra and therefore the representation structure; the eigenvalue equations label a particular basis. You can rotate to another basis without changing the algebra. This distinction is useful when comparing matrix spin calculations with spherical-harmonic wave-function calculations.

## Orbital angular momentum in coordinates

For a particle moving in ordinary space, orbital angular momentum comes from the cross product of position and momentum. Its coordinate representation is a first-order differential operator that differentiates only angular variables. The radial coordinate therefore commutes with every component of orbital angular momentum. A potential that depends only on radius also commutes with them, while a potential that singles out an axis generally preserves at most the projection about that axis.

The eigenfunctions of the squared orbital operator and its vertical component are spherical harmonics. Their azimuthal dependence follows immediately from the vertical component: an eigenfunction with projection label (m) carries a phase that winds (m) times around the axis. Single-valued scalar wavefunctions require an integer winding number. Regularity at the poles and normalizability then restrict the magnitude label and produce the familiar finite range of projections.

The coordinate calculation supplies details that the abstract algebra leaves open. The algebra permits integer and half-integer irreducible representations. Scalar functions on the sphere realize only the integer ones. Half-integer representations require spinor-valued objects whose transformation law is sensitive to the double cover of the rotation group. The distinction concerns the representation space, not a modification of the commutation relations.

Spherical harmonics also show how parity enters. Under spatial inversion, a harmonic with orbital label $\ell$ acquires a factor $(-1)^\ell$. Angular momentum itself is an axial vector and does not reverse under parity, but the orbital wavefunction carries this definite parity. The result becomes useful when determining which matrix elements of position or other polar-vector operators vanish.

## Degeneracy, symmetry, and broken symmetry

If a Hamiltonian is rotationally invariant, it commutes with every component of total angular momentum and with the total square. Applying a ladder operator to an energy eigenstate then produces another eigenstate with the same energy, unless the result vanishes. Every irreducible multiplet is consequently degenerate across its projection labels. The degeneracy is enforced by symmetry rather than by an accidental equality of separately computed energies.

An external field along one axis reduces the symmetry. The projection about that axis can remain conserved, but raising and lowering no longer commute with the Hamiltonian. Energy may then depend on the projection, as in a Zeeman splitting. A completely anisotropic interaction can remove even that remaining continuous rotational symmetry. Asking which rotations survive is often faster than computing a long list of commutators independently.

Rotational invariance can coexist with additional degeneracy. The Coulomb Hamiltonian has levels of different orbital angular momentum at the same energy, a fact not explained by ordinary rotations alone. Its conserved Runge–Lenz vector enlarges the symmetry. By contrast, rotational symmetry alone guarantees equality only among the projection states within a fixed angular-momentum multiplet.

## Vector operators and selection rules

The commutator of angular momentum with a vector operator states that the operator's components rotate into one another. Position, momentum, and angular momentum itself all satisfy this relation, although angular momentum behaves differently under parity. Matrix elements of a vector operator between angular-momentum eigenstates inherit the same addition rules used for combining a state with spin one.

This observation underlies the dipole selection rules of atomic spectroscopy. The angular dependence of a position matrix element can be separated from a reduced matrix element that contains radial and dynamical information. The angular factor vanishes unless the initial angular momentum, the vector rank, and the final angular momentum satisfy a triangle condition. Projection labels must also add appropriately. Symmetry can therefore declare a transition forbidden before any radial integral is attempted.

The word forbidden means that the matrix element vanishes for the stated interaction and symmetry assumptions. A higher-multipole interaction, spin-dependent term, or symmetry-breaking field may allow the same pair of levels to communicate weakly. Selection rules organize approximations; they are not universal bans detached from a Hamiltonian.

For hand calculations, it helps to separate three questions. First determine the representation labels allowed by the algebra. Next identify which of those labels remain conserved by the Hamiltonian. Only then choose a basis and compute matrix elements. Mixing these stages is a common source of claims that a projection is conserved merely because it appears in the notation.

Angular-momentum coherent states provide one final bridge to classical geometry. They are obtained by rotating a highest-weight state and have the smallest transverse uncertainty allowed for their mean angular momentum. For large quantum number, their relative fluctuations shrink and the expectation value behaves like a directed classical vector. Generic eigenstates of one projection do not share that localization; the classical-vector picture is a property of a selected family of states, not of the algebra alone.
