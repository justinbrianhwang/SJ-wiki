# Main-Group Elements

The main-group elements occupy the $s$ and $p$ blocks. Their chemistry is the clearest test of whether periodic trends have become reasoning tools rather than arrows memorized on a table. A group supplies a common valence pattern, but size, bond energy, lattice energy, solvation, and accessible oxidation states decide what a particular element actually does.

## Valence patterns and their limits

For representative elements, the group number encodes the outer-shell configuration. Group 1 has $ns^1$, group 2 has $ns^2$, and groups 13 through 18 fill $ns^2np^1$ through $ns^2np^6$. The noble-gas configuration is often reached by losing electrons on the left side of the table, gaining electrons near the right edge, or sharing electrons in the middle.

| Family | Valence shell | Common oxidation states | Characteristic theme |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group 1, alkali metals | $ns^1$ | $+1$ | reactive metals and strongly basic compounds |
| Group 2, alkaline earths | $ns^2$ | $+2$ | harder, less reactive metals than group 1 |
| Group 13, boron family | $ns^2np^1$ | $+3$, also $+1$ down the group | electron deficiency and a nonmetal-to-metal transition |
| Group 14, carbon family | $ns^2np^2$ | $-4$ to $+4$; $+2$ favored lower down | strong covalent networks, chains, and increasing metallic character |
| Group 15, pnictogens | $ns^2np^3$ | $-3$, $+3$, $+5$ | multiple oxidation states and basic hydrides |
| Group 16, chalcogens | $ns^2np^4$ | $-2$, $+4$, $+6$ | oxides, sulfides, and extensive redox chemistry |
| Group 17, halogens | $ns^2np^5$ | $-1$; positive states except for F | diatomic oxidants and halide salts |
| Group 18, noble gases | $ns^2np^6$ | $0$; positive states for heavier members | low reactivity, with exceptions for polarizable atoms |

These oxidation states are bookkeeping possibilities, not guarantees of stability. The maximum positive state often matches the number of valence electrons, but heavier $p$-block elements increasingly retain the $ns^2$ pair. This inert-pair effect makes states two units below the group maximum more stable down groups 13–16: thallium favors $+1$ more than aluminum does, and lead favors $+2$ more than carbon does. Relativistic effects and the energetic cost of using the $s$ pair both contribute; the old phrase “inert pair” describes the observation rather than a complete mechanism.

## The metallic side: groups 1 and 2

Alkali metals have low first ionization energies and form $M^+$ ions. Their reactivity with water generally increases down the group because the valence electron lies farther from the nucleus and is removed more readily. The reaction is

$$
\mathrm{2M(s)+2H_2O(l)\rightarrow2MOH(aq)+H_2(g)}.
$$

The hydroxides are strong bases. The trend is not determined by ionization energy alone: atomization of the metal, hydration of the cation, and product lattice energies all enter a full energy cycle. The observed direction nevertheless agrees with increasingly easy electron loss.

Group 2 metals require removal of two electrons. They are generally harder, denser, and less reactive than neighboring group 1 metals. Their oxides have formula $MO$ and their halides $MX_2$. Solubility trends show why simple slogans fail. Group 2 hydroxides tend to become more soluble down the group, whereas the sulfates tend to become less soluble. Both dissolution processes balance lattice disruption against hydration; ionic size changes those terms at different rates.

Hydrogen sits above group 1 because it has a $1s^1$ configuration, but it is not an alkali metal under ordinary conditions. It forms a covalent $\mathrm{H_2}$ molecule, can lose an electron in acid-base bookkeeping, and can gain one to form hydride, $\mathrm{H^-}$. Its placement signals an electron count, not full chemical membership.

### Worked example: sodium in water

Suppose $4.60\ \mathrm{g}$ of sodium reacts completely with excess water. First balance the equation:

$$
\mathrm{2Na+2H_2O\rightarrow2NaOH+H_2}.
$$

Convert sodium mass to moles:

$$
n(\mathrm{Na})=\frac{4.60\ \mathrm{g}}{22.99\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}=0.200\ \mathrm{mol}.
$$

The coefficient ratio is one mole of hydrogen for two moles of sodium:

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2})=0.200\times\frac{1}{2}=0.100\ \mathrm{mol}.
$$

Therefore

$$
m(\mathrm{H_2})=(0.100)(2.016)=0.202\ \mathrm{g}.
$$

The small product mass is consistent with conservation of mass because most of the sodium and water mass appears in sodium hydroxide, not hydrogen gas.

## Across the p block: structure changes the chemistry

Boron is a covalent network-forming metalloid, while the heavier group 13 elements are metals. Boron compounds are often electron deficient. In $\mathrm{BF_3}$, boron has only six electrons in its Lewis structure and can accept a lone pair from a Lewis base. Aluminum chloride also has appreciable covalent character and can form molecular or dimeric species, a reminder that a metal–nonmetal pair is not automatically well described as fully ionic.

Carbon's small size permits strong C–C bonds and effective overlap for multiple bonds. It forms long chains, rings, sheets, and three-dimensional networks. Silicon also forms four bonds readily, but Si–Si multiple bonding is less favorable and strong Si–O bonds dominate much terrestrial chemistry. Down group 14, tin and lead become metallic; the $+2$ state becomes increasingly important relative to $+4$.

Nitrogen and phosphorus share $ns^2np^3$ but differ sharply. Strong $\mathrm{N\equiv N}$ bonding makes elemental nitrogen kinetically unreactive under ordinary conditions. Phosphorus instead forms several molecular or network allotropes with P–P single bonds. Nitrogen readily makes $p\pi$–$p\pi$ multiple bonds; phosphorus more often builds single-bond frameworks or bonds to oxygen. Ammonia is more basic than phosphine in water because the nitrogen lone pair is more available for proton bonding.

Oxygen occurs mainly as paramagnetic $\mathrm{O_2}$, while sulfur has several allotropes, including rings such as $\mathrm{S_8}$. Oxygen's small atoms form strong multiple bonds; sulfur's larger atoms favor S–S single bonds and can support higher coordination numbers in compounds. Hydride acidity rises down group 16: water is a weak acid, while $\mathrm{H_2S}$ is more acidic because the larger, weaker H–S bond is easier to break.

Allotropy is a structural phenomenon. Diamond and graphite contain only carbon, but their connectivity differs. Diamond's tetrahedral network is hard and electrically insulating. Graphite contains strong bonded sheets with delocalized electrons and weaker attractions between sheets, giving directional conductivity and easy cleavage. Composition alone cannot predict a solid's properties.

## Oxides trace the metal–nonmetal transition

Oxides provide a compact survey across a period. Strongly metallic elements form ionic oxides that react with water or acids as bases. Nonmetal oxides are usually covalent and often react with water or bases as acids. Near the boundary, amphoteric oxides react with either acids or bases.

For example, sodium oxide is basic:

$$
\mathrm{Na_2O+H_2O\rightarrow2NaOH}.
$$

Sulfur trioxide is an acidic oxide:

$$
\mathrm{SO_3+H_2O\rightarrow H_2SO_4}.
$$

Aluminum oxide is amphoteric. With acid it consumes protons:

$$
\mathrm{Al_2O_3+6H^+\rightarrow2Al^{3+}+3H_2O}.
$$

With strong base it forms soluble aluminate species; the exact written form depends on solvent and convention. “Amphoteric” does not mean neutral. It means the oxide can participate on either side of acid-base chemistry.

Acidity can also change with the central atom's oxidation state. Higher oxidation states withdraw electron density more strongly through oxygen, often producing more acidic oxides and oxoacids. Thus an element name alone does not fix acid-base behavior.

## Halogens: oxidizing power and displacement

Elemental fluorine, chlorine, bromine, and iodine occur as diatomic molecules. They accept electrons according to

$$
\mathrm{X_2+2e^-\rightarrow2X^-}.
$$

Oxidizing strength decreases from fluorine to iodine. Bond energies, electron affinity, and hydration all contribute, so the trend cannot be reduced to electronegativity alone. The reducing strength of halide ions runs in the opposite direction: iodide is more easily oxidized than bromide, which is more easily oxidized than chloride.

### Worked example: chlorine and bromide

To test whether chlorine displaces bromide, write the reduction favored for the stronger oxidant:

$$
\mathrm{Cl_2+2e^-\rightarrow2Cl^-}.
$$

Bromide supplies the electrons:

$$
\mathrm{2Br^-\rightarrow Br_2+2e^-}.
$$

Adding and canceling electrons gives

$$
\mathrm{Cl_2(aq)+2Br^-(aq)\rightarrow2Cl^-(aq)+Br_2(aq)}.
$$

The reverse reaction is not favored under comparable standard aqueous conditions. A common error is to say “chloride is more reactive than bromide” without naming the direction of electron transfer. Chlorine is the stronger oxidizing agent; bromide is the stronger reducing agent.

Fluorine differs from the other halogens. It essentially takes only the $-1$ oxidation state in its compounds because no element attracts shared electrons more strongly. Chlorine, bromine, and iodine can have positive oxidation states when bonded to oxygen or fluorine.

## Why noble gases react at all

A filled valence shell gives noble gases high ionization energies and little tendency to accept an electron. “Inert” is still too absolute. Larger noble-gas atoms are more polarizable and hold their outer electrons less tightly. Xenon forms stable compounds with highly electronegative elements, especially fluorine and oxygen. Helium and neon remain far less reactive because ionizing or strongly polarizing their compact electron clouds is energetically costly.

The old octet picture correctly anticipates low reactivity but does not prove impossibility. Chemical stability depends on the energy of products relative to reactants and on the activation barrier connecting them. A closed shell is one large contribution to that balance.

## First-member anomalies and diagonal resemblance

The first member of a main group often departs from the pattern below it. A second-period atom has no filled inner shell between its valence electrons and the nucleus, so it is unusually small and often more electronegative. Its compact $2p$ orbitals overlap well enough to support strong multiple bonds, but the atom cannot exceed four electron-pair directions without severe crowding. Nitrogen therefore forms strong N=N and N≡N bonds, while phosphorus favors networks of single bonds. Oxygen forms O=O readily, whereas sulfur commonly forms rings and chains.

Small size also raises charge density. Lithium is the least typical alkali metal: its ion is strongly hydrated, several of its salts show solubilities closer to magnesium salts than to sodium salts, and its nitride is readily formed directly. This resemblance between lithium and magnesium is called a diagonal relationship. Beryllium similarly resembles aluminum in its amphoteric compounds and substantial covalent character. The diagonal comparison is not a new law; moving down reduces electronegativity while moving right increases it, so the changes partly compensate.

Hydrides expose several trends at once. Across period 2, the sequence $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$, and $\mathrm{HF}$ moves toward more polar E–H bonds. Their shapes and lone pairs alter intermolecular forces and acid-base behavior. Down a group, E–H bonds generally weaken as the central atom grows. Acid strength of the hydrogen halides therefore rises from HF to HI even though fluorine is most electronegative: breaking the exceptionally strong H–F bond is energetically costly, while the larger halide ions better accommodate negative charge.

### Worked comparison: assigning likely formulas

For a simple ionic compound of magnesium and nitrogen, start from the common ions $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{N^{3-}}$. Electrical neutrality requires the least common multiple of the charge magnitudes, six. Three magnesium ions supply $+6$ and two nitride ions supply $-6$, giving $\mathrm{Mg_3N_2}$. The subscripts come from charge balance, not from the group numbers copied mechanically.

For sulfur and oxygen, that ionic procedure is inappropriate because both are nonmetals and form covalent oxides. Oxidation states help distinguish compounds rather than dictate a single formula. $\mathrm{SO_2}$ places sulfur formally at $+4$; $\mathrm{SO_3}$ places it at $+6$. Both exist, and the available oxidation states reflect the p-block element's redox chemistry. Before “crossing charges,” decide whether discrete ions are a sound structural model.

## Checks for periodic-trend arguments

- Specify the species. A trend for elemental halogens may reverse for their anions.
- Separate thermodynamics from rate. Nitrogen's reaction with hydrogen is favorable under suitable conditions but slow without activation and catalysis.
- Do not treat a family as chemically uniform. First-row elements often differ because they are small, strongly electronegative, and unable to accommodate high coordination numbers.
- When a solubility or redox trend seems anomalous, include all major energy terms. Ionization energy alone rarely settles a condensed-phase reaction.
- Oxidation state is formal bookkeeping. It does not assert that a covalently bonded atom carries that integer charge.
