# Continuous Random Variables and Uniform Laws

Moving from a discrete sample space to a continuum changes how probabilities are represented. A discrete variable assigns mass to points. A continuous variable with a density assigns mass to intervals by integration. The value of the density at one point is therefore not a probability; it is a local rate of probability per unit of the horizontal axis.

The uniform law is the cleanest model in which to learn this distinction. Its density is constant, so every probability calculation reduces to a ratio of lengths. It also gives a standard source of randomness: many other continuous distributions can be generated by transforming a uniform variable.

## From point masses to densities

A random variable $X$ is **absolutely continuous** if there is a nonnegative function $f_X$ such that, for every suitable set $A$,

$$
P(X\in A)=\int_A f_X(x)\,dx.
$$

The function $f_X$ is a probability density function, or PDF. It must obey

$$
f_X(x)\ge 0,
\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\,dx=1.
$$

The adjective “continuous” is often used informally for “has a density,” but the precise distinction matters. A continuous cumulative distribution function need not have a density. The standard Cantor distribution is the usual counterexample. Most elementary models—uniform, normal, exponential, beta—are absolutely continuous, so densities cover the common cases without covering every possible continuous law.

For an interval, probability is area:

$$
P(a<X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx.
$$

An interval of zero length has zero area. Hence, for every fixed $a$,

$$
P(X=a)=\int_a^a f_X(x)\,dx=0.
$$

There is no contradiction in saying that $X$ must take some value even though every specified value has probability zero. Probability zero does not mean logical impossibility. It means that the event is negligible under the probability measure. The same phenomenon appears when a point chosen uniformly from a line segment must land somewhere, while no point was favored beforehand.

If $f_X$ is continuous near $a$, then for small positive $h$,

$$
P(a\le X\le a+h)
=\int_a^{a+h}f_X(x)\,dx
\approx f_X(a)h.
$$

The approximation identifies the units as well. If $X$ is measured in seconds, $f_X$ has units of inverse seconds. A density can exceed $1$; only its integral over an event must lie between $0$ and $1$. For example, the uniform density on $[0,1/4]$ has height $4$.

## The CDF carries the whole distribution

The cumulative distribution function is defined for every random variable:

$$
F_X(x)=P(X\le x).
$$

For a variable with density $f_X$,

$$
F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt.
$$

At every point where the CDF is differentiable,

$$
F_X'(x)=f_X(x).
$$

Thus the CDF accumulates probability and the density describes its instantaneous rate of increase. A valid CDF is nondecreasing and right-continuous, approaches $0$ at $-\infty$, and approaches $1$ at $+\infty$. A jump of size $p$ at $a$ records an atom $P(X=a)=p$. An absolutely continuous CDF has no jumps.

Endpoints require less ceremony than they do in the discrete case. If $X$ has a density, then

$$
P(a<X<b)=P(a\le X<b)=P(a<X\le b)=P(a\le X\le b),
$$

because adding or removing either endpoint changes the event only by a probability-zero singleton. This equivalence fails as soon as an endpoint has positive point mass.

The CDF also provides interval probabilities without returning to the density each time:

$$
P(a<X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a).
$$

Its complement, the survival function,

$$
\overline F_X(x)=P(X>x)=1-F_X(x),
$$

is often more natural for waiting times and reliability calculations. For a nonnegative random variable, the mean can be recovered from its tail:

$$
E[X]=\int_0^\infty P(X>t)\,dt,
$$

when the mean is finite. Geometrically, a realized value $X=x$ contributes one unit to the indicator of $X\gt t$ for every $t$ between $0$ and $x$. Integrating those indicators over $t$ gives $x$; taking expectations gives the formula. For $X\sim U(0,b)$, the survival function is $1-t/b$ on $[0,b]$, and the area of that triangle is $b/2$, the familiar mean.

Expectation itself needs an existence condition. It is safe when $E[\vert X\vert ]$ is finite. Writing a formal difference between an infinite positive contribution and an infinite negative contribution does not define a mean. Uniform variables avoid the issue because their support is bounded, so all polynomial moments exist.

## Why the uniform density has its particular height

Let $X$ be uniform on $[\alpha,\beta]$, where $\alpha\lt \beta$. “Uniform” means equal-length subintervals receive equal probabilities. A constant density $c$ on the interval expresses that condition. Normalization determines $c$:

$$
1=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}c\,dx=c(\beta-\alpha),
$$

so

$$
f_X(x)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{\beta-\alpha}, & \alpha\le x\le\beta,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

The geometry is a rectangle whose area is one:

```text
height = 1 / (beta - alpha)

            +-------------------------+
            |                         |
            |                         |
------------+-------------------------+------------
          alpha                     beta

          area = width x height = 1
```

Integrating from the left endpoint gives the CDF:

$$
F_X(x)=
\begin{cases}
0, & x<\alpha,\\
\dfrac{x-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}, & \alpha\le x\le\beta,\\
1, & x>\beta.
\end{cases}
$$

Consequently, if $[a,b]$ lies inside the support,

$$
P(a\le X\le b)=\frac{b-a}{\beta-\alpha}.
$$

Equal probability for equal lengths distinguishes the uniform law from a statement about equal point probabilities. There are uncountably many points in an interval, so assigning the same positive mass to each one would make the total mass infinite. Assigning zero mass to each point is compatible with total mass one because countable additivity does not say that an uncountable union must have probability equal to an uncountable sum. Length, formalized by Lebesgue measure, is the reference measure behind the uniform density.

The continuous uniform law also differs from a discrete uniform law. A fair die is uniform on six points, with probability $1/6$ at each point. Rounding a $U(0,6)$ variable down produces a discrete uniform variable on $\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ because each unit interval has the same length. Rounding to the nearest integer without adjusting endpoints does not give a uniform result: the bins at the two ends have only half the width of the interior bins. Whenever a continuous measurement is binned, the bin boundaries determine the induced discrete probabilities.

### Uniform phase and wraparound

Uniformity behaves especially well under translation on a circle. Let $U\sim U(0,L)$ and define

$$
V=(U+c)\bmod L
$$

for a fixed shift $c$. Then $V$ is again uniform on $[0,L)$. Any interval of length $d$ after the shift has a preimage consisting of one interval or two pieces whose total length is $d$, so its probability remains $d/L$.

The modulo operation is important. The ordinary translated variable $U+c$ is uniform on $[c,L+c]$, not on the original interval. Wraparound identifies the two endpoints and treats the interval as a circle. Random clock phase, angle, and position within a periodic schedule use this model. It also states the correct translation-invariance property: a bounded uniform density cannot remain unchanged under every translation on the real line, but uniform measure on a finite circle can remain unchanged under rotations.

If the shift is itself random and independent of $U$, the result remains uniform after conditioning on the shift. For every fixed value of the shift, the conditional distribution is uniform; averaging identical uniform conditional laws changes nothing. This observation is often used to “randomize the origin” of a periodic process without altering its phase distribution.

For an interval that extends outside the support, clip it first. A compact formula is

$$
P(a\le X\le b)
=\frac{\max\!\left(0,\min(b,\beta)-\max(a,\alpha)\right)}{\beta-\alpha}.
$$

## Deriving the mean and variance

The expectation of a function of an absolutely continuous variable is

$$
E[g(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)f_X(x)\,dx,
$$

provided the integral exists in the required sense. Taking $g(x)=x$ gives the mean. For $X\sim U(\alpha,\beta)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X]
&=\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}x\,dx\\
&=\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{\alpha}^{\beta}\\
&=\frac{\beta^2-\alpha^2}{2(\beta-\alpha)}\\
&=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The result agrees with symmetry: the balance point of a constant-density interval is its midpoint. For the variance, first compute the second moment:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^2]
&=\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}x^2\,dx\\
&=\frac{\beta^3-\alpha^3}{3(\beta-\alpha)}\\
&=\frac{\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2}{3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)
&=E[X^2]-E[X]^2\\
&=\frac{\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2}{3}
-\frac{(\alpha+\beta)^2}{4}\\
&=\frac{(\beta-\alpha)^2}{12}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The variance depends only on the interval width. Translating an interval changes its center but not its spread. Stretching its width by a factor $c$ multiplies variance by $c^2$.

This separation into center and width makes the uniform laws a location-scale family. Writing $m=(\alpha+\beta)/2$ and $w=\beta-\alpha$ gives

$$
X=m+wV,
\qquad V\sim U(-1/2,1/2).
$$

The standardized variable $V$ contains the common shape; $m$ chooses location and $w$ chooses scale. Standardization therefore removes the parameters:

$$
\frac{X-E[X]}{\operatorname{SD}(X)}
=\sqrt{12}\,V
\sim U(-\sqrt3,\sqrt3).
$$

The standardized support has endpoints $\pm\sqrt3$, mean zero, and variance one. This representation is useful when comparing shapes across distributions, since location and measurement units no longer obscure the comparison.

| Law | Density on its support | Mean | Variance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $U(0,1)$ | $1$ | $1/2$ | $1/12$ |
| $U(-a,a)$ | $1/(2a)$ | $0$ | $a^2/3$ |
| $U(\alpha,\beta)$ | $1/(\beta-\alpha)$ | $(\alpha+\beta)/2$ | $(\beta-\alpha)^2/12$ |

## Rescaling a standard uniform variable

Suppose $U\sim U(0,1)$ and define

$$
X=\alpha+(\beta-\alpha)U.
$$

As $U$ runs from $0$ to $1$, $X$ runs linearly from $\alpha$ to $\beta$. For $\alpha\le x\le\beta$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_X(x)
&=P(X\le x)\\
&=P\!\left(\alpha+(\beta-\alpha)U\le x\right)\\
&=P\!\left(U\le\frac{x-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}\right)\\
&=\frac{x-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the CDF of $U(\alpha,\beta)$. The same conclusion follows from the change-of-variables formula. Since

$$
u=\frac{x-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha},
\qquad
\left|\frac{du}{dx}\right|=\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha},
$$

the transformed density is

$$
f_X(x)=f_U\!\left(\frac{x-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}\right)
\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}
=\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}
$$

on $[\alpha,\beta]$. The derivative factor is essential: stretching the axis lowers the density so total area remains one.

More generally, let $F$ be a continuous, strictly increasing CDF and let $U\sim U(0,1)$. Then

$$
X=F^{-1}(U)
$$

has CDF $F$, because

$$
P(X\le x)
=P(F^{-1}(U)\le x)
=P(U\le F(x))
=F(x).
$$

This inverse-CDF construction is a basic simulation method. In the other direction, if $X$ has a continuous CDF $F$, then $F(X)$ is uniform on $[0,1]$. Strict monotonicity can be relaxed by using a generalized inverse, but flat portions of a CDF require a little care.

### Quantiles and percentiles

For $0\lt p\lt 1$, a $p$-quantile is a value $q_p$ at which the CDF reaches probability $p$. With a continuous strictly increasing CDF,

$$
q_p=F^{-1}(p).
$$

The uniform quantile is especially simple:

$$
q_p=\alpha+p(\beta-\alpha).
$$

Thus the median is the midpoint, the first quartile lies one quarter of the interval width from the left endpoint, and the $90$th percentile lies nine tenths of the way across. Quantiles are stable summaries when moments are unhelpful or overly sensitive to extreme values. The Cauchy distribution, for example, has a median and all ordinary percentiles even though its mean does not exist.

Percentile ranks explain why uniform variables appear even when the observed measurements are far from uniform. If heights in a population have continuous CDF $F$, the raw height $X$ is concentrated around typical human heights, but the rank $F(X)$ is uniform. Ten percent of the population falls in each decile by construction. Ties or atoms complicate the exact statement because a jump in $F$ causes many observations to share a percentile.

### When the transformation folds the line

The simple Jacobian formula above assumed a one-to-one transformation. A non-monotone function may have several input values leading to the same output, and their density contributions must be added. Let $U\sim U(-1,1)$ and $Y=U^2$. For $0\le y\le1$,

$$
F_Y(y)=P(U^2\le y)=P(-\sqrt y\le U\le\sqrt y)=\sqrt y.
$$

Differentiation gives

$$
f_Y(y)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt y},
\qquad 0<y<1.
$$

The density becomes unbounded near zero, yet it is valid: its integral over $[0,1]$ is one. Both $+\sqrt y$ and $-\sqrt y$ map to $y$, and the squaring map compresses intervals near zero. This example gives two useful checks against common intuition: transforming a uniform variable need not preserve uniformity, and an infinite density at an endpoint need not imply infinite probability there.

## Worked example: normalizing an unfamiliar density

Suppose $X$ has density

$$
f_X(x)=
\begin{cases}
cx, & 0\le x\le 2,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

First determine $c$. Normalization gives

$$
1=\int_0^2 cx\,dx
=c\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^2
=2c,
$$

so $c=1/2$. The CDF inside the support is

$$
F_X(x)=\int_0^x \frac{t}{2}\,dt=\frac{x^2}{4},
\qquad 0\le x\le2.
$$

Now calculate the probability that $X$ lies between $1/2$ and $3/2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\!\left(\frac12\le X\le\frac32\right)
&=F_X\!\left(\frac32\right)-F_X\!\left(\frac12\right)\\
&=\frac{(3/2)^2}{4}-\frac{(1/2)^2}{4}\\
&=\frac{9}{16}-\frac{1}{16}\\
&=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

The answer happens to equal the interval length divided by the support length, but the distribution is not uniform. For example,

$$
P(0\le X\le1)=\frac14,
\qquad
P(1\le X\le2)=\frac34.
$$

The increasing density puts three times as much mass in the right half as in the left half.

## Worked example: waiting for a scheduled train

Trains arrive exactly every $12$ minutes. A passenger’s arrival time relative to the schedule is modeled as uniform over one cycle. Let $T\sim U(0,12)$ be the number of minutes since the previous train. The waiting time is

$$
W=12-T.
$$

Because this is a reflection of a uniform interval, $W\sim U(0,12)$. To derive it rather than rely on symmetry, for $0\le w\le12$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_W(w)
&=P(W\le w)\\
&=P(12-T\le w)\\
&=P(T\ge12-w)\\
&=1-F_T(12-w)\\
&=1-\frac{12-w}{12}\\
&=\frac{w}{12}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The chance of waiting between $3$ and $7$ minutes is

$$
P(3\le W\le7)=\frac{7-3}{12}=\frac13.
$$

The expected wait and standard deviation are

$$
E[W]=6,
\qquad
\operatorname{SD}(W)=\sqrt{\frac{12^2}{12}}=\sqrt{12}\approx3.46.
$$

The uniform assumption belongs to the model, not to the word “random.” It is reasonable when passenger arrivals have no systematic relation to a deterministic schedule. If train arrivals follow a Poisson process, the waiting time is exponential. If passengers time their walk to the timetable, arrival phase will generally be nonuniform.

## Worked example: the larger of two uniform draws

Let $U_1$ and $U_2$ be independent $U(0,1)$ variables, and set

$$
M=\max(U_1,U_2).
$$

Although each input is uniform, the maximum is not. For $0\le m\le1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_M(m)
&=P(M\le m)\\
&=P(U_1\le m,\ U_2\le m)\\
&=P(U_1\le m)P(U_2\le m)\\
&=m^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

where independence permits the product in the third line. Differentiating gives

$$
f_M(m)=2m,
\qquad 0<m<1.
$$

The mean follows from direct integration:

$$
E[M]=\int_0^1 m(2m)\,dm
=2\left[\frac{m^3}{3}\right]_0^1
=\frac23.
$$

For a numerical check,

$$
P(M>0.8)=1-F_M(0.8)=1-0.64=0.36.
$$

One draw exceeds $0.8$ with probability $0.2$, but two chances to exceed the threshold raise the probability to $0.36$, not $0.4$, because the event that both exceed it would otherwise be counted twice.

## Modeling cautions

- A density value and a point probability are different objects. The statement $f_X(1)=2$ carries no claim that a probability equals $2$.
- A formula is not a density until it is nonnegative and integrates to one over its full support. Solving for a normalizing constant should come before computing probabilities.
- The support matters. Integrating the uniform density beyond $[\alpha,\beta]$ produces nonsense unless the integration limits are clipped.
- Strict and non-strict inequalities are interchangeable only when the boundary points have probability zero. Mixed distributions may contain both a density and atoms.
- Under $Y=a+bX$, the mean changes to $a+bE[X]$, while the variance becomes $b^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$. The translation $a$ has no effect on variance.
- “Chosen at random” does not automatically mean uniform. A mechanism or symmetry argument must justify why equal-length regions should have equal chances.
