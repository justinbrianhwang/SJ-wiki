# Martingales, Risk-Neutral Probability, and Black-Scholes

The link between a fair gamble and an option price is conditional expectation. A martingale models a quantity whose best prediction, given the information currently available, is its present value. Mathematical finance applies that idea after two adjustments: use discounted prices, and take expectations under a probability measure chosen to rule out arbitrage rather than to forecast observed frequencies.

That distinction is easy to blur. The physical measure describes a modeler's beliefs about returns. A risk-neutral measure is a pricing device. The two can assign different probabilities to the same event while agreeing on today's traded prices.

## Fairness depends on what is known

Let $(\mathcal F_n)_{n\geq 0}$ be a filtration: an increasing sequence of information sets,

$$
\mathcal F_0\subseteq\mathcal F_1\subseteq\cdots.
$$

A process $(X_n)$ is adapted when $X_n$ is known at time $n$. It is a **martingale** with respect to $(\mathcal F_n)$ if $E[\vert X_n\vert ]\lt \infty$ and

$$
E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n
$$

for every $n$. Iterating conditional expectation gives

$$
E[X_m\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n,\qquad m\geq n.
$$

The filtration is part of the assertion. If $Y_1,Y_2,\ldots$ are independent, mean-zero increments and

$$
S_n=S_0+\sum_{k=1}^nY_k,
$$

then $S_n$ is a martingale for the natural filtration generated by the observed increments. It need not remain one for an enlarged filtration that reveals tomorrow's increment today.

Another standard construction is

$$
M_n=E[Z\mid\mathcal F_n]
$$

for an integrable terminal quantity $Z$. The tower property proves the martingale condition:

$$
E[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]
=E[E[Z\mid\mathcal F_{n+1}]\mid\mathcal F_n]
=M_n.
$$

One can read $M_n$ as a sequence of revised forecasts. New evidence moves the forecast, but before seeing that evidence there is no predictable direction to the revision.

Processes satisfying $E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]\geq X_n$ are **submartingales**; reversing the inequality gives **supermartingales**. The naming follows the direction in which conditional means may drift, not a claim that every sample path increases or decreases.

## Stopping without looking ahead

A random time $T$ is a **stopping time** if the event $\{T\leq n\}$ is determined by $\mathcal F_n$ for every $n$. “Stop when the account first reaches $70$” qualifies. “Stop one step before its eventual maximum” does not, because it asks for future data.

For a bounded stopping time $T\leq N$ and an integrable martingale,

$$
E[X_T]=E[X_0].
$$

A short proof writes the stopped value through its increments:

$$
X_T=X_0+\sum_{k=1}^{N}(X_k-X_{k-1})1_{\{T\geq k\}}.
$$

The indicator is known at time $k-1$. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[(X_k-X_{k-1})1_{\{T\geq k\}}]
&=E\left[1_{\{T\geq k\}}E[X_k-X_{k-1}\mid\mathcal F_{k-1}]\right]\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Summing proves the result. Other optional-stopping versions allow unbounded $T$, but they need hypotheses such as bounded stopped values, uniformly integrable martingales, or suitable bounds on increments and $E[T]$. “A martingale cannot be beaten by any stopping rule” is too broad without one of these controls. Doubling strategies fail where unlimited credit, unbounded losses, or non-integrable stopping times enter.

### Worked example: exit from an interval

A fair random walk starts at $X_0=50$, moves by $+1$ or $-1$ with equal probability, and stops upon first reaching $40$ or $70$. Let

$$
T=\inf\{n\geq0:X_n\in\{40,70\}\}
$$

and $p=P(X_T=70)$. The stopped process $X_{n\wedge T}$ stays between $40$ and $70$, so bounded optional stopping applies. Hence

$$
E[X_T]=E[X_0]=50.
$$

There are only two terminal values:

$$
\begin{aligned}
70p+40(1-p)&=50,\\
30p+40&=50,\\
p&=\frac{1}{3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

From a starting point $x\in[a,b]$, the same argument gives

$$
P_x(X_T=b)=\frac{x-a}{b-a}.
$$

The expected duration needs a second martingale. For a unit-step fair walk, $X_n^2-n$ is a martingale because

$$
E[X_{n+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n^2+1.
$$

Stopping it yields $E[T]=E[X_T^2]-X_0^2$. Here,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X_T^2]
&=70^2\left(\frac13\right)+40^2\left(\frac23\right)\\
&=2700,\\
E[T]&=2700-2500=200.
\end{aligned}
$$

The walk has a one-third chance of reaching the upper boundary and a mean stopping time of 200 steps.

## One-period risk-neutral pricing

The finance argument already appears in a one-period binomial market. Suppose a stock costs $S_0$ now and will be either $S_0u$ or $S_0d$ after one period. A risk-free dollar grows by $R=e^{rT}$. To exclude an immediate arbitrage, the risk-free growth factor must lie strictly between the two stock returns:

$$
d<R<u.
$$

Define

$$
q=\frac{R-d}{u-d}.
$$

Then $0\lt q\lt 1$ and

$$
qS_0u+(1-q)S_0d=RS_0.
$$

After discounting by $R$, the stock has conditional mean $S_0$ under the probability assigning mass $q$ to the up state. That is the one-period risk-neutral measure.

### Worked example: replicate a call

Take $S_0=100$, $u=1.2$, $d=0.8$, $R=1.05$, and a call strike $K=100$. Its terminal payoff is $20$ in the up state and $0$ in the down state. First compute

$$
q=\frac{1.05-0.8}{1.2-0.8}=0.625.
$$

Risk-neutral valuation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_0
&=R^{-1}\left(q\cdot20+(1-q)\cdot0\right)\\
&=\frac{12.5}{1.05}\\
&\approx11.9048.
\end{aligned}
$$

The same number follows from replication. Hold $\Delta$ shares and $B$ dollars in the bank. Match both terminal states:

$$
\begin{aligned}
120\Delta+1.05B&=20,\\
80\Delta+1.05B&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtracting gives $\Delta=0.5$. The second equation gives $B=-38.0952$. The portfolio costs

$$
100(0.5)-38.0952=11.9048.
$$

Any other call price would permit buying the cheaper of the call and its replicating portfolio while selling the dearer one. The physical probability of an up move never entered the calculation.

## Discounted prices and a change of measure

Choose the money-market account $B_t=e^{rt}$ as numeraire. Under a risk-neutral measure $Q$, each traded asset's discounted price

$$
\widetilde S_t=\frac{S_t}{B_t}=e^{-rt}S_t
$$

is a martingale. For a replicable payoff $G$ at time $T$, its time-zero value is

$$
V_0=e^{-rT}E_Q[G].
$$

An event contract paying $1_A$ has price $e^{-rT}Q(A)$. With $T=2$, $r=0.05$, and $Q(A)=0.30$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_0
&=e^{-0.05\cdot2}(0.30)\\
&=e^{-0.10}(0.30)\\
&\approx0.27145.
\end{aligned}
$$

The measure $Q$ depends on the numeraire. With the money-market account it is called risk-neutral because every traded asset earns the risk-free rate in conditional expectation before discounting. It does not say investors are indifferent to risk.

In a complete market every suitable contingent claim can be replicated, and the risk-neutral measure is unique. In an incomplete market there may be several such measures; no-arbitrage alone then gives a range of admissible prices rather than one number.

## From geometric Brownian motion to the terminal law

The Black-Scholes model assumes that under $Q$ a non-dividend-paying stock obeys

$$
dS_t=rS_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t^Q,
$$

where $\sigma\gt 0$ is constant. Applying Itô's formula to $\log S_t$ supplies the correction that is often missed:

$$
d\log S_t=\left(r-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)dt+\sigma\,dW_t^Q.
$$

Integrating gives

$$
S_T=S_0\exp\left(\left(r-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T+\sigma\sqrt T\,Z\right),
\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).
$$

Thus $\log S_T$ is normal and $S_T$ is lognormal. The negative half-variance term ensures

$$
E_Q[S_T]=S_0e^{rT},
$$

because $E[e^{\sigma\sqrt T Z}]=e^{\sigma^2T/2}$.

For a European call, $G=(S_T-K)^+$. Split the expectation at $S_T=K$:

$$
C_0=e^{-rT}\left(E_Q[S_T1_{\{S_T>K\}}]-KQ(S_T>K)\right).
$$

The threshold $S_T\gt K$ is equivalent to $Z\gt -d_2$, where

$$
d_2=\frac{\log(S_0/K)+(r-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T},
\qquad
d_1=d_2+\sigma\sqrt T.
$$

Normal symmetry gives $Q(S_T\gt K)=\Phi(d_2)$. Completing the square in the first truncated-normal integral gives

$$
e^{-rT}E_Q[S_T1_{\{S_T>K\}}]=S_0\Phi(d_1).
$$

Therefore

$$
C_0=S_0\Phi(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_2).
$$

For a stock paying continuous dividend yield $q_d$, replace $S_0$ by $S_0e^{-q_dT}$ in the first term and replace $r$ by $r-q_d$ inside $d_1$ and $d_2$.

### Worked example: a one-year call

Let $S_0=100$, $K=105$, $r=0.04$, $\sigma=0.20$, and $T=1$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_1
&=\frac{\log(100/105)+(0.04+0.20^2/2)}{0.20}\\
&=\frac{-0.048790+0.060000}{0.20}\\
&\approx0.05605,\\
d_2&=0.05605-0.20=-0.14395.
\end{aligned}
$$

Using $\Phi(0.05605)\approx0.52235$ and $\Phi(-0.14395)\approx0.44276$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_0
&=100(0.52235)-105e^{-0.04}(0.44276)\\
&\approx52.235-44.658\\
&\approx7.58.
\end{aligned}
$$

The price respects the elementary bounds

$$
\max(0,S_0-Ke^{-rT})\leq C_0\leq S_0.
$$

As $T\downarrow0$, the formula tends to $(S_0-K)^+$. As $\sigma\downarrow0$, it tends to the discounted payoff of the deterministic risk-neutral terminal price $S_0e^{rT}$.

## What the formula assumes

Black-Scholes uses continuous trading, frictionless borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate, constant volatility and rate, no jumps, and a continuously varying price. European exercise matters: an American claim adds an optimal-stopping problem. For a non-dividend-paying stock, early exercise of an American call is suboptimal under the usual assumptions, but early exercise can matter for puts and dividend-paying stocks.

Observed returns have volatility that changes through time and tails heavier than the lognormal model predicts. Market option prices converted back through the formula produce an implied-volatility surface rather than one constant $\sigma$. The formula remains a common quoting and comparison convention, even when traders use richer models for risk.

The call payoff is convex. Holding its risk-neutral mean fixed, a mean-preserving increase in dispersion raises its expected payoff by Jensen's inequality. That accounts for positive Black-Scholes vega; it is a statement about convexity, not a forecast that the stock's mean return increases with volatility.

## The same price from dynamic hedging

Risk-neutral expectation and replication are two views of one argument. Let an option value be a smooth function $V(t,S)$. Under a physical stock model

$$
dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t,
$$

Itô's formula gives

$$
dV=\left(V_t+\mu SV_S+\frac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\right)dt
+\sigma SV_S\,dW_t.
$$

Form a portfolio holding one option and short $\Delta=V_S$ shares. Its random $dW_t$ term cancels. Over an instant the hedged portfolio is locally riskless, so no arbitrage requires it to earn the risk-free rate. Rearranging gives the Black-Scholes partial differential equation

$$
V_t+\frac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}+rSV_S-rV=0.
$$

The physical drift $\mu$ has disappeared. The terminal condition contains the contract: $V(T,S)=(S-K)^+$ for a call. Solving this boundary-value problem gives the same formula as the discounted expectation under $Q$.

The hedge ratio $\Delta=V_S$ is the option's delta. For a Black-Scholes call without dividends,

$$
\Delta=\Phi(d_1).
$$

It ranges from nearly zero for a far out-of-the-money call to nearly one for a far in-the-money call. Delta itself changes with the stock and time, so replication calls for rebalancing. The model assumes continuous, costless rebalancing. Actual hedges are discrete and face transaction costs, jumps, liquidity limits, and volatility-estimation error. A quoted formula value therefore does not eliminate hedging risk.

The second derivative $V_{SS}$ is gamma. It measures curvature and controls how quickly delta changes. A large gamma makes a position more sensitive to discrete rebalancing error. It is also the term through which volatility enters the PDE: convexity converts quadratic price variation into option value.

## Put-call parity and bounds as model checks

A European call and put with the same strike and maturity satisfy

$$
C_0-P_0=S_0-Ke^{-rT}
$$

for a non-dividend-paying stock. To see it, compare two portfolios. A call plus a bond paying $K$ at maturity is worth $\max(S_T,K)$. A put plus one share is also worth $\max(S_T,K)$. Equal terminal payoffs must have equal current prices in an arbitrage-free market.

Suppose the call in the earlier example costs $7.58$. Then the corresponding put price is

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_0
&=C_0-S_0+Ke^{-rT}\\
&=7.58-100+105e^{-0.04}\\
&\approx8.46.
\end{aligned}
$$

Parity is valuable even outside Black-Scholes because it uses replication, not a distributional assumption. Violations beyond trading costs indicate inconsistent quotes.

Simple dominance arguments also give bounds. A call cannot be worth more than the stock, and a put cannot exceed the present value of its strike. For nonnegative rates,

$$
\max(0,S_0-Ke^{-rT})\leq C_0\leq S_0,
$$

$$
\max(0,Ke^{-rT}-S_0)\leq P_0\leq Ke^{-rT}.
$$

These checks should precede numerical sophistication. A sign error in $d_1$, an incorrect time unit, or mixing annual and daily volatility can still produce a plausible-looking number that violates an elementary bound.

## What changes outside the basic market

Interest rates may vary with maturity, so a zero-coupon bond price replaces the single factor $e^{-rT}$. Discrete dividends require explicit treatment of payment dates; a continuous yield is only an approximation. Foreign-exchange options treat the foreign money-market account much like a dividend yield. Futures options use a different underlying and numeraire, leading to related but distinct formulas.

American options introduce a choice of exercise time. Their value is an optimal-stopping problem: at each state, compare immediate exercise with the conditional value of continuing. The discounted American value process is tied to a supermartingale rather than a plain terminal expectation. Binomial trees expose this recursion directly and remain useful when early exercise or discrete cash flows make the closed form unavailable.

Risk-neutral probabilities also need not be unique. If some risks cannot be hedged with traded assets, several measures may make discounted prices martingales. Selecting one price then requires additional modeling, calibration, preferences, or a specified hedging criterion. The slogan “discount the risk-neutral expectation” hides that completeness assumption.

## A note on mathematical versus market probability

The notation can tempt one to read $Q(S_T\gt K)=\Phi(d_2)$ as the market's best forecast that the call finishes in the money. It is the probability under the money-market pricing measure. A physical forecast uses the real-world drift and possibly a different return model. The two probabilities answer different questions.

Even $\Phi(d_1)$ is not the same probability under $Q$; it arises from a stock-weighted change of measure in the truncated expectation and also equals call delta in the basic model. Remembering the roles prevents a common interpretation error: $d_1$ and $d_2$ are standardized thresholds produced by two different weighted integrals, not two competing forecasts.

Calibration runs the pricing logic backward. Given a market call price and all other inputs, solve for the volatility that makes the Black-Scholes value match. This implied volatility summarizes the quote in common units. It is not necessarily a forecast of realized volatility, and different strikes and maturities normally yield different values. The resulting surface records where the constant-volatility model fails while retaining its convenient pricing vocabulary.

## Numerical check

```python
from math import erf, exp, log, sqrt

def normal_cdf(x):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + erf(x / sqrt(2.0)))

def black_scholes_call(S0, K, r, sigma, T):
    if T == 0:
        return max(S0 - K, 0.0)
    if sigma == 0:
        return exp(-r * T) * max(S0 * exp(r * T) - K, 0.0)
    d1 = (log(S0 / K) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * T) / (sigma * sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sqrt(T)
    return S0 * normal_cdf(d1) - K * exp(-r * T) * normal_cdf(d2)

print(black_scholes_call(100, 105, 0.04, 0.20, 1))
```

The explicit boundary branches avoid division by zero and state the limiting model. Production pricing must also settle day-count conventions, dividend treatment, rate curves, and numerical accuracy in the normal CDF.
